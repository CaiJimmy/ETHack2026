    Words used here

    Market value
    The price of one share multiplied by the number of shares. It tells you what the whole company is worth on the stock market.
    Revenue
    All the money a company gets from sales in one year, before any costs.
    EBITDA
    Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. It is the profit from normal operations before financing and accounting costs. We use it to see how much profit a carbon cost can remove.
    ESG risk score
    A score from Sustainalytics. It measures how much of a company's environmental, social and governance risk is not managed. A low score is good. Higher than 40 is "severe".
    Direct CO2 emissions
    Greenhouse gases that come out of the company's own sites, for example power plants and refineries. This is "Scope 1". The EPA only counts US sites that release more than 25,000 tonnes a year.
    Altman Z-score
    A formula from a company's balance sheet that predicts bankruptcy risk. Below 1.8 is the danger zone. Above 3 is safe. It does not work well for banks.
    Piotroski F-score
    Nine yes/no checks of financial strength, for example "profit went up" and "debt went down". 9 is the best.
    Implied temperature
    If every company had this company's emission plan, this is how much the planet would warm. 1.5 °C is the Paris goal.
    Controversy flags
    Six topics: environment, social, customers, human rights, labour, governance. A flag is any colour other than green. 0 flags is best.
    Sector percentile
    Where a company is in its sector on one metric. 100 means it is the best in the sector. 0 means it is the worst.

    The scenario

    Tomorrow the world agrees to reach net zero fast. Carbon gets a price. You manage $1 billion. The controls below set the rules of your fund.

    How the fund is built

    1. Start with the index. Each company gets the same weight as in the S&P 500. A big company gets a big weight.
    2. Add the carbon cost. Carbon price × direct emissions = a yearly cost. We compare that cost to the company's profit (EBITDA). This gives "profit at risk".
    3. Move money away from risk. A company with high profit at risk gets a smaller weight. The slider "How strongly" sets how much smaller.
    4. Apply the emission limit. If the fund still releases too much CO2, we halve the weight of the dirtiest company. We repeat this until the limit holds.

    What the model does not know

    • Emissions come from US sites only, and only sites above 25,000 tonnes a year. A company with no such site gets a carbon cost of zero. This is a gap, not a fact. Banks and software companies have emissions in their supply chain that we do not count.
    • We assume the company pays the full carbon cost. In reality some of it goes to customers as higher prices.
    • "Active share" is a simple stand-in for tracking error.

    Sources for this tab

      Words used here

      Index
      The S&P 500: the 500 largest listed US companies. A fund that copies the index holds every company in proportion to its market value.
      Weight
      The share of the fund's money in one company. 2% weight of $1 billion is $20 million.
      Carbon price
      A cost per tonne of CO2. Governments set it with a tax or a permit market.
      EBITDA
      Profit from operations, before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. If EBITDA is not known, we use 15% of revenue.
      Profit at risk
      Yearly carbon cost divided by EBITDA. 30% means the carbon price would take 30% of the profit. We cap it at 100%.
      Emission intensity
      Tonnes of CO2 for each $1 million of revenue. It lets you compare a small company with a big one.
      Active share
      How different the fund is from the index, from 0% (identical) to 100% (nothing in common).

            Words used here

            CO2 per person
            All CO2 the country releases in a year, divided by its population. Production-based: it counts what is made in the country, not what the country imports.
            Renewable electricity
            The share of electricity that comes from wind, sun, water, and other renewable sources.
            GDP per person
            The value of everything a country produces in a year, divided by its population.
            Head office
            The city where the company has its main office. We place each logo there. It does not show where the company's factories or emissions are.

            What we mean by "sustainable"

            A company is sustainable when it can keep its business in a world that puts a price on carbon, enforces labour and safety law, and holds the board responsible. We measure this in three pillars: Environment, Social, Governance. We always compare a company with the other companies in its sector. A bank and a power company cannot be compared on emissions.

            The companies

            The 500 companies in the S&P 500 index as of September 2026. The list comes from a market screener. It has a few OCR errors in company names. Three companies have two share classes; we keep one row for each company.

            How the score is made

            1. For each metric, we rank the company against the other companies in its sector. The result is a percentile from 0 to 100. We flip the scale for metrics where low is good.
            2. Each pillar score is the average of the percentiles in that pillar. A missing metric is left out. It is not replaced with an average.
            3. The total score is the weighted average of the three pillars. You set the weights with the sliders.
            4. A company with no data in any pillar has no score. The Explore tab shows how many companies this affects.

            Why we chose these sources

            • EPA GHGRP is the only large, free, audited, facility-level emissions dataset. Companies must report by law. It covers US sites above 25,000 tonnes a year. We only use direct emissions. The EPA file also lists "supplier" quantities (fuel sold to others). Those are not emissions from the site, and we exclude them.
            • Sustainalytics ESG Risk is a well-known rating. A free copy for the S&P 500 is on Kaggle. It gives the Social and Governance pillars, which have no free raw data source.
            • Global Corporate ESG and Financial Dataset (Kaggle) adds the fourth pillar, financial resilience (Altman Z, Piotroski F), plus each company's decarbonisation target, implied temperature and six controversy flags. Its "involvement" screens (coal, weapons, tobacco) are empty for 9 in 10 companies, so we do not use them.
            • Yahoo Finance gives profit (EBITDA) and employees, which we need for the fund model. It also gives the head-office city, which we place on the globe with OpenStreetMap.
            • World Sustainability Dataset (World Bank and Our World in Data, via Kaggle) gives the country numbers on the World Map: CO2, renewable electricity, GDP, life expectancy. The latest year is 2018.

            Limits you should know

            • Sector ranking hides one fact: the best power company still releases much more CO2 than the worst bank.
            • Emissions in the supply chain (Scope 3) are not counted. For banks and retailers this is where most emissions are.
            • The Sustainalytics copy is from 2024. The market data is from 2025 and 2026. The emissions are from 2023.
            • A Kaggle dataset of sustainability-report scores was also available. Its scores copy the Sustainalytics numbers, so we do not use it as a second source.
            • Matching EPA parent companies to tickers uses company names. We checked every match by hand; the list is in the repository.
